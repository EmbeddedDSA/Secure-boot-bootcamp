How the course runs

  • Questions before explanations. Every topic opens with questions you answer first. Type TELLME to get a straight answer after you have tried.
  • Every concept gets a lab. You build it, then break it. The labs are the course.
  • Every session ends with a grill of 8 to 12 questions, asked one at a time and scored 0 to 3. Anything under 3 returns after 3, 10 and 30 days until it scores 3 twice in a row.
  • Every session starts with whatever repetition questions are due.
  • All documents are offline HTML; the dashboard (index.html) is regenerated every session.

Sixteen-week timeline

Week 1 begins 2026-10-14. The weeks are a target, not a promise: a module does not end because the calendar says so, it ends when the gate is passed.

Mastery gates

A module is passed, and the next one unlocks, only when all three hold:

  1. every lab in the module is complete and its code runs (and passes CI);
  2. the module's grill average is 2.5 or higher;
  3. a 20-question end-of-module oral exam, asked one at a time, averages 2.5 or higher.

Scale: 0 = blank, 1 = wrong direction, 2 = right idea but missing detail, 3 = would pass an interview.

Module 1 — Threat models and the root of trust

Week 1

Concepts. Assets worth protecting; attacker tiers from remote-only to lab-grade physical and the cost of defending each; the bootstrapping problem; boot ROM and one-time-programmable fuses; why fuses hold a hash of the public key; verified versus measured boot; the five roots of trust in NIST SP 800-193; lifecycle states and why each transition is permanent; authenticated debug for returned units.

Labs.

  • lab-00: RSA and EC key pairs with openssl; RSA-PSS sign and verify a dummy image; flip one byte and watch verification fail; SHA-256 of the DER public key (the fuse value).
  • lab-01: a ~200-line C parser and verifier for a simple signed image header; then add a time-of-check-to-time-of-use bug and exploit it.

Deliverable. docs/module-01 complete; a written threat model for a device of your choosing.

Module 2 — Cryptography for firmware engineers

Weeks 2–3

Concepts. Hash functions and collision resistance; HMAC and why hash(key||message) is broken; why secure boot must be asymmetric; RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 versus PSS; ECDSA, nonce reuse and deterministic signatures; Ed25519; AES modes, ECB, AES-GCM and GCM nonce reuse; HKDF; key wrapping; X.509 chains and revocation; constant-time comparison; LMS and XMSS.

Labs.

  • SHA-256 from scratch in C, checked against openssl; HMAC on top of it.
  • Length-extension attack against a naive hash MAC.
  • Recover an ECDSA private key from two signatures sharing a nonce.
  • Constant-time memcmp, with measured timing against the naive one.
  • Three-level certificate chain with openssl, and a C verifier for it.

Deliverable. Crypto reference document; from-scratch SHA-256 and HMAC in labs/ with CI.

Module 3 — The open-source security stack

Week 4

Concepts. mbedTLS and PSA Crypto; OpenSSL 3.x EVP and providers; wolfSSL and FIPS; TF-A; TF-M; MCUboot; U-Boot verified boot; EDK II and UEFI Secure Boot; coreboot and vboot; OP-TEE; Trusty; tpm2-tss and tpm2-tools; swtpm; dm-verity; IMA and EVM; RAUC, SWUpdate, Uptane, SUIT; PKCS#11, SoftHSM, Parsec; fuzzers and sanitizers; meta-arm, meta-security, meta-tpm.

Labs.

  • One operation through the PSA Crypto API (mbedTLS) and again through OpenSSL 3 EVP; compare.
  • Read MCUboot image validation end to end; document every check in order.
  • Boot a software TPM under QEMU with swtpm and read a PCR with tpm2-tools.

Deliverable. Stack map document, as inline SVG.

Module 4 — Secure boot mechanics

Weeks 5–6

Concepts. Image headers and manifests and what the signature must cover; how a boot ROM verifies, step by step; anti-rollback counters; key slots and revocation; what happens when a signing key leaks; ARM TBBR chain of trust; TF-A certificate structure; UEFI PK, KEK, db, dbx; Qualcomm, Nvidia Tegra and AMD PSP approaches compared.

Labs.

  • Your own signed image format in C: header, manifest, version, signature, signing tool, verifier.
  • Anti-rollback with a file standing in for a fuse.
  • Tamper test suite; then I attack your format and you fix at least two weaknesses.

Deliverable. Signed image format in labs/ with CI; honest comparison against TF-A.

Module 5 — ARM security architecture

Week 7

Concepts. EL0 to EL3; secure and non-secure worlds and the NS bit; SCR_EL3; TZASC and TZC; SMC and SMCCC; Cortex-M TrustZone and why it differs; PAC, BTI, MTE; CCA and Realms.

Labs.

  • Bare-metal AArch64 that boots at EL3, drops to EL1 and SMCs back up, under QEMU.
  • Decode SCR_EL3 and SCTLR_EL1 bit by bit.
  • A secure memory region and a refused non-secure access.

Deliverable. Exception-level and world-switch document with register-state SVG diagrams.

Module 6 — The ARM boot chain in practice

Week 8

Concepts. TF-A BL1, BL2, BL31, BL32, BL33; configuring the chain of trust; PSCI and the runtime monitor; U-Boot FIT, signature nodes, key in the control device tree; why DT and command line must be signed; handing a verified rootfs to Linux.

Labs.

  • TF-A for QEMU with trusted board boot; watch each stage verify the next.
  • Corrupt BL33 and see BL2 refuse it.
  • U-Boot verified boot with a signed FIT; tamper the DT and see it fail; the unsigned-DT init attack.

Deliverable. Annotated boot log and reproducible build documentation.

Module 7 — Trusted execution environments

Week 9

Concepts. OP-TEE architecture; TA loading, verification, isolation; GlobalPlatform APIs; secure storage and RPMB; shared memory attack surface; TEE vs TPM vs secure element vs HSM.

Labs.

  • OP-TEE on QEMU alongside Linux.
  • A TA that derives a key from the HUK and signs; a normal-world client.
  • Secure storage Linux cannot read; a vulnerable TA that trusts a normal-world pointer, exploited.

Deliverable. Working TA in labs/ and a TEE attack-surface document.

Module 8 — Hardware security blocks

Week 10

Concepts. TRNGs and entropy assessment; why weak RNG breaks everything; PUFs and error correction; crypto accelerators and side channels; key ladders; the HUK; per-device per-purpose derived keys; secure counters.

Labs.

  • Statistically test an entropy source and catch a weakened one.
  • Key-derivation hierarchy in C from a simulated HUK; show what breaks when the root is shared.

Deliverable. Key hierarchy implementation and design rationale.

Module 9 — Attestation and device identity

Week 11

Concepts. PCRs and extend; quotes; sealing; event log replay; DICE; RATS; entity attestation tokens; factory identity injection.

Labs.

  • swtpm: measure a boot, quote, and write a verifier.
  • Seal a secret to PCRs; watch unseal fail after a change.
  • Three-layer DICE chain in C, verified.

Deliverable. Attestation verifier in labs/ and an architecture document. Patent prior-art search runs this week.

Module 10 — Secure update and key management

Week 12

Concepts. A/B updates and atomic switchover; rollback protection in the field; CI signing without the key on the build machine; key ceremonies; key compromise response; SBOMs; vulnerability response; Uptane.

Labs.

  • SoftHSM + PKCS#11 signing pipeline wired into this repo's CI.
  • A/B update with rollback protection and a downgrade-rejection test.
  • SBOM for a lab, and a scan of it.

Deliverable. HSM-backed signing pipeline in CI.

Module 11 — Attacking the chain

Week 13

Concepts. Voltage, clock and EM fault injection; glitch-resistant coding; SPA and DPA; constant time as a requirement; debug port attacks; TOCTOU in boot; boot ROM memory safety; real case studies.

Labs.

  • Simulated instruction skipping against your Module 4 verifier; count accepted bad images.
  • Harden it, re-run, measure the improvement.
  • Timing attack on a naive password check. Real glitch with ChipWhisperer if you have one.

Deliverable. Glitch-resistance report with before-and-after numbers.

Module 12 — Standards and certification

Week 14

Concepts. PSA Certified; SESIP; Common Criteria attack potential; NIST SP 800-193; FIPS 140-3; ISO/SAE 21434; UNECE R155 and R156; EU Cyber Resilience Act; a silicon vendor's SDL.

Labs.

  • ISO 21434-style TARA for one of your labs.
  • Map the capstone against SP 800-193 and find where it fails.

Deliverable. Compliance mapping for the capstone.

Module 13 — Capstone and interview craft

Weeks 15–16

Concepts. TF-A chain of trust, OP-TEE TA holding a device key, signed FIT with signed DT, dm-verity rootfs, anti-rollback, measured boot into swtpm, server-side attestation verifier. One reproducible script, CI, HTML docs.

Labs.

  • The full chain on qemuarm64, built by one script and tested in CI.

Deliverable. Design document, threat model, honest known-limitations, and a five-minute spoken walkthrough.

Interview layer

  • From Week 2: weekly interview preparation. The question bank grows with every question asked, each with your score and a spoken-style model answer. Company notes are researched and cited.
  • From Week 6: one full 45-minute mock interview per week: warm-up, deep technical, a whiteboard design problem, one hard push-back, and "tell me about a time you found a security problem". Transcripts saved with per-answer scores.
  • From Week 12: adversarial mocks: interruptions, challenges to correct answers, and questions outside what we covered, to train saying "I don't know, here is how I'd reason about it" instead of bluffing.
  • Also drilled: the two-minute project pitch, drawing a boot chain fast and legibly, and asking the panel good questions.

Hardware plan

Buy nothing until Week 8. QEMU covers Modules 1 to 7. In Week 8 we check current availability and delivered prices in India and compare boards in three price bands against seven criteria: programmable OTP fuses, upstream TF-A, OP-TEE support, a public secure boot flow and signing tool, a recoverable download mode, mainline U-Boot and Linux, and delivered cost. Candidates: STM32MP1, NXP i.MX, TI AM62/AM64 high-security parts, and for Cortex-M TrustZone the STM32H5/U5, LPC55S69 or nRF5340. The ChipWhisperer-Nano is the cheapest route to a real glitch in Module 11. Raspberry Pi and most Allwinner/Rockchip boards cannot teach this subject.

Patent workstream

Runs in a separate private repository. Nothing about the candidate mechanism is ever written in this public repository. No commitment to a candidate before Module 9, when a proper prior-art search is run and written up. Then: prototype with measurements, invention disclosure, and a clear point at which to involve a patent attorney.

Schedule risks

  • Module 2 is the steepest climb for someone with no crypto background. It has two weeks; it may need them both and more.
  • Module 6 in one week is tight. TF-A and U-Boot builds are quick, but first-time chain-of-trust debugging is not. Builds over 30 minutes will be flagged in advance with a smaller alternative.
  • The capstone depends on everything before it. Any slip in Modules 4 to 10 lands here.
